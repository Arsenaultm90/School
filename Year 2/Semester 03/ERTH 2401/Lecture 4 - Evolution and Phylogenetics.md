
#### Definition of biological evolution
Biological evolution refers to the cumulative changes that occur in a population over time — also called "descent with modification." Evolution does not occur at the individual level; it only occurs at the population level, through interbreeding and the passing of genetic changes across generations.

#### Key terms
**Adaptation**
	How organisms become specialized to suit their environment.
**Inheritance**
	Passing features (characters) from parents to offspring.
**Variation**
	Differences in features seen among individuals within a species.
**Natural selection**
	The differential survival and reproduction of individuals; advantageous traits increase in frequency, disadvantageous ones decrease


#### Genotype vs. phenotype
Evolution occurs at the genetic (genotypic) level — genes mutate and recombine during reproduction. But what we actually observe, as paleontologists, is the morphology — the shape and form of the organism. This expressed form is phenotypic evolution. In this course, we study the phenotypic evolution of dinosaur groups, even though the underlying mechanism is genetic change.

All dinosaur groups share a common bipedal ancestor resembling _Coelophysis_. Phenotypic evolution then diverges toward sauropods, tyrannosaurs, ceratopsians, etc.

### Species Turnover 
~99.999% of all species that have ever lived are now extinct. Vertebrate species in the fossil record typically persist for about 2 million years before being replaced. Even "living fossils" like coelacanths have evolved at the genetic level, even if their morphology appears static. Evolution is inevitable at geological time scales.

## Phylogenetic systematics

#### What is phylogenetics?
Phylogenetics = _phylum_ (group of related organisms) + _genesis_ (origin). It is the study of evolutionary origins and relationships among organisms. This falls under the broader science of systematics — the organization and classification of life into groups. Humans are naturally "hardwired" to group things: the same intuition that separates cars from trucks from trains applies to separating lizards from crocodiles from dinosaurs.

#### Linnaeus and the Linnaean system
Carl Linnaeus (18th century Swedish naturalist) formalized a nested hierarchy of life based on overall similarity. His system gave us the ranks still in use today: Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. Example for humans: Animalia → Chordata → Mammalia → Primates → Hominidae → _Homo sapiens_. However, the Linnaean system relied on qualitative judgments. Modern phylogenetics replaced this with quantitative, character-based analysis tied explicitly to evolutionary ancestry.

#### Important vocabulary
**Taxon / taxa**
A named group of organisms (singular/plural). Often interchangeable with "group" in this course.

**Clade**
A group consisting of a common ancestor and all of its descendants. Always monophyletic.

**Node**
A branching point on a cladogram. Can be named (e.g., Mammalia). Each node represents a hypothetical common ancestor.

**Character**
A distinctive, definable feature of an organism used in phylogenetic analysis. Must be quantifiable (present/absent, or coded numerically).

**Morphology**
The overall shape and form of an organism — what we observe as paleontologists.

**Taxonomy**
The practice of naming and classifying organisms into groups.

## Homology vs. Analogy

#### Homology
Similarity due to shared ancestry. The structure or feature has the same evolutionary origin, regardless of its current function. Example: the forelimb of pterosaurs, birds, bats, and humans all contain the same bones (humerus, radius, ulna, digits) in the same positions — all derived from the same tetrapod ancestor, even though one uses it to fly, another to write. We use homologous structures to identify shared derived characters for cladograms.
Used in cladogram construction

#### Analogy (convergent evolution)
Similarity in function, but not in evolutionary origin or anatomical structure. Example: the hindlimb of a human and the jumping leg of a grasshopper look similar and do similar things, but have completely different evolutionary origins. Analogous structures must not be used as shared derived characters in phylogenetic analysis — they would incorrectly group unrelated organisms.

## Cladograms

#### What a cladogram is
A cladogram is a hierarchical branching diagram showing shared diagnostic characters among taxa. It is a hypothesis of evolutionary relationships — not a confirmed evolutionary tree. The characters chosen influence the result. Organisms that share the most derived characters are placed closest together; those sharing fewer are placed further apart (closer to the base). Rotating branches at a node does not change the relationships — nodes can rotate 360°.

#### How to read the textbook cladograms
In the course textbook, each node has a small red dot (the branching point) and a red slash mark below it. The slash and its caption list the shared derived characters (synapomorphies) that define the clade above that node. Example: the node "Mammalia" — red slash lists warm-bloodedness and fur/hair as the defining characters for carnivores, bears, gorillas, and humans above it.

#### How cladograms are built — the character matrix
Researchers compile a matrix: taxa as rows, characters as columns. Each character is coded as present (1), absent (0), or unknown (?). The matrix is fed into computer software that generates the most parsimonious cladogram — the one requiring the fewest evolutionary changes. Software used by researchers: PAUP (Phylogenetic Analysis Using Parsimony) and TNT. Students do not need to use or know these programs — you will only need to read the resulting cladograms.

Ryan prefers TNT for speed; PAUP is slower with large matrices but has specific advantages. Different researchers may code the same characters differently — a key source of disagreement between competing published cladograms.

## Types of groups

#### Monophyletic group (clade)
The only valid clade type in this course.
A group that includes a single common ancestor and all of its descendants. Everything within the bounding node belongs. Example: Reptilia includes lizards, crocodilians, non-avian dinosaurs, and birds — birds are therefore reptiles. Monophyletic groups are nested within each other: Aves is within Dinosauria, which is within Archosauria, which is within Reptilia, which is within Amniota, which is within Tetrapoda, which is within Vertebrata.

#### Paraphyletic group
Includes a common ancestor and some, but not all, of its descendants. A researcher has excluded some members for a non-phylogenetic reason. Not a true clade. Rarely used in this course.
Not a clade

#### Polyphyletic group
A group not defined by a single common ancestor — organisms grouped by geography, behaviour, or diet rather than ancestry. Not a true clade. Will not appear in this course.
Not a clade

## Advanced character terminology

**Apomorphy**
A derived character state unique to a particular taxon or group.

**Synapomorphy**
A derived character shared by a common ancestor and all its descendants. This is what unites members of a clade.

**Plesiomorphy**
An ancestral (primitive) character state inherited from deep in the lineage. Less useful for defining clades.

**Symplesiomorphy**
A plesiomorphy shared by two or more taxa — shared primitive character. Unlikely to appear on exams but mentioned in lecture.

## Worked Examples — Linnaean classification applied

#### _Triceratops horridus_
Clade: Dinosauria (dinosaurs) → Order: Ornithischia (ornithischians) → Clade: Ceratopsia (ceratopsians) → Family: Ceratopsidae (ceratopsids) → Genus: _Triceratops_ → Species: _horridus_. Closest sister group to ceratopsians: Pachycephalosauria (dome-headed dinosaurs).

#### _Tyrannosaurus rex_
Clade: Dinosauria (dinosaurs) → Order: Saurischia (saurischians) → Clade: Theropoda (theropods) → Family: Tyrannosauridae (tyrannosaurids) → Genus: _Tyrannosaurus_ → Species: _rex_. These colloquial terms (theropods, tyrannosaurs, etc.) will be used throughout the course.
