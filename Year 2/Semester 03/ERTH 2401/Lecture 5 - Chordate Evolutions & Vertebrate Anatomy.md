## Part 1: Chordate Evolution

### The Cladogram Framework

The course now moves fully into cladogram mode. Clades are presented from least to most derived (bottom to top). Named nodes represent clades — everything above a node belongs to that clade. Key clades covered today:

**Chordata → Vertebrata → Gnathostomata → Osteichthyes → Sarcopterygii → Tetrapoda → Amniota → Diapsida**

All life is monophyletic, united by shared DNA, RNA, cell membranes, and chemical pathways — meaning everything evolved from a single common ancestor.

---

### Phylum Chordata

**Earliest known chordate:** _Pikaia_ (~510 million years ago, Burgess Shale)

**Three diagnostic characters of Chordata** _(exam-relevant — know all three)_:

1. **Pharyngeal gill slits** — openings into the throat/pharynx; water enters the mouth, passes over the gills, delivering oxygen to the body
2. **Notochord** — a stiff rod running along the back; the precursor to the vertebral column
3. **Nerve cord** — runs from the brain down the back, branching to organs and appendages

---

### Urochordata (Sea Squirts)

- Adult form (_e.g., Ciona_) looks like a soft bag on the ocean floor with two openings (one draws water in, one expels it)
- **Key point:** The juvenile/larval form is a free-swimming tadpole-like creature — it looks like the adult form of a cephalochordate
- Juveniles eventually settle and metamorphose into the adult body shape

---

### Cephalochordata (Amphioxus)

- _Amphioxus_ is the classic example — look up the "Amphioxus Song" on YouTube
- More derived than urochordates; retains pharyngeal gill slits, notochord, and nerve cord, **plus:**
    - Muscle segmentation
    - Branching nerves
    - Blood vessels
    - Hormone and enzyme systems
- Lives buried tail-first in ocean sediment with tentacled head filtering particles from the water

---

### Vertebrata

**Defining character _(most exam-relevant)_:** Bones are organized into specific named elements (humerus, radius, femur, etc.)

Other characters of Vertebrata (6 total, but only the above is exam-required):

- Neural crest cells (important in developing brain/neural tissue)
- Differentiated cranial nerves
- Functional eyes
- Kidneys
- New hormonal systems
- Mouth parts / jaw structures

**General trend in vertebrate evolution:** Progressive _loss_ (or fusion) of bones as you move through more derived clades. Fishes have more bones than tetrapods; our single lower jaw bone (dentary) is actually two fused bones, and jaw bones in early mammals became inner ear bones.

---

### Agnatha (Jawless Fish)

- First appeared in the **Cambrian**
- Examples: hagfish and lamprey (living); ostracoderms (extinct)
- Lack paired fins; retain notochord in adult form
- **Fun fact:** Hagfish can secrete a viscous slime that essentially coagulates the water around them as a defense mechanism

**Ostracoderms** — armored jawless fish, primarily Silurian–Late Devonian. Heavily armored, less maneuverable than later bony fish.

---

### Origin of Jaws (Gnathostomata)

**Gnathostomata** = vertebrates with true articulating jaws. Includes sharks, elephants, humans — everything with a working jaw.

**Hypothesis for jaw evolution:**

- Jawless fish had many gill arches (bony/cartilaginous supports for gill slits)
- Over evolutionary time, the **first pair of gill arches** was co-opted into the upper and lower jaw
- More gill arch material was later incorporated, making jaws increasingly sophisticated
- **Teeth** evolved from **scales** — dermal tissue on sharks was co-opted to the front of the jaw; earliest vertebrate fossil evidence is often shark scales

---

### Chondrichthyes (Cartilaginous Fish)

- Includes sharks, rays, chimaeras
- Skeleton formed only by **cartilage** (no endochondral bone)
- Can produce some dermal bone: teeth, denticles (skin scales), fin spines
- First unequivocal fossil evidence from the **Silurian**, primarily as scales

---

### Osteichthyes (Bony Fish)

- Includes everything with a true bony (endochondral) skeleton — includes you and me
- First appear in the **late Silurian**
- Divided into:
    - **Actinopterygii** (ray-finned fishes) — ~99% of all fish alive today
    - **Sarcopterygii** (lobe-finned fishes) — the group from which tetrapods evolved

---

### Actinopterygii (Ray-Finned Fishes)

- Defined by bony rays in fins
- Enormously diverse — the vast majority of living fish
- Complex, highly ossified skeletons

---

### Sarcopterygii (Lobe-Finned Fishes)

- Famous living representative: **coelacanth** (_Latimeria_)
    - Thought extinct after Late Cretaceous until rediscovered in the early 1900s
    - Morphologically similar to fossil forms — stable deep-ocean environment = low selective pressure to change body shape
    - _Note:_ Evolution still occurred at the genetic/molecular level even without obvious morphological change
- **Key development:** The tetrapod forelimb evolved from lobe-fin precursors
    - Coelacanth and humans share **homologous bones**: humerus, radius, ulna, and digit elements
    - Bony fin rays were gradually reduced and lost on the path to tetrapods

---

### The Fish-to-Land Transition

**Key comparison — _Eusthenopteron_ (lobe-finned fish) vs. _Canastega_ (early amphibian):**

|Feature|_Eusthenopteron_|_Canastega_|
|---|---|---|
|Shoulder girdle|Attached to skull (no neck)|Separate from skull — true neck|
|Head mobility|Limited|Mobile|
|Pelvic girdle|Not attached to vertebral column|Attached to vertebral column|
|Weight support|Cannot support weight on land|Can support weight on land|

**Key transitional animal: _Tiktaalik_** (from northern Canada; fossils housed in Ottawa)

- Called a "fishapod" (fish + tetrapod) — a mosaic of characters
- Fish-like: gills, scales, bony fin rays (not toes)
- Tetrapod-like: functional wrist joint, mobile neck, **lungs**, and **ribs**
    - Ribs are critical for life on land — without them, soft tissue collapses and prevents breathing
- **Lungs** are modified swim bladders — originally used for buoyancy control, then co-opted for air breathing

**Driving factor for moving onto land:** Devonian pools were likely stagnant and low in oxygen; moving between pools offered access to more oxygenated water and better food sources.

**Another transitional animal:** _Qikiqtania_ (close relative of _Tiktaalik_, also from northern Canada)

- Slightly different story — appears to have secondarily returned to water (like early whale ancestors)
- Limb bones modified back into paddle-like structures

**Early tetrapod digit count:** The earliest tetrapods had more than 5 digits (up to 7–8 have been found). The pentadactyl (5-digit) condition became fixed later through natural selection.

> Recommended reading: _Your Inner Fish_ by Dr. Neil Shubin (discoverer of _Tiktaalik_); currently on sale at Chapters.

---
### Tetrapoda

**Defining character:** Four limbs capable of supporting weight or enabling mobility on land

---
### Amniota

**Defining character:** Production of the **amniotic egg** (hard-shelled egg)

- The **amnion** membrane prevents water loss while allowing O₂ to diffuse in and CO₂ to diffuse out
- This freed tetrapods from needing to return to water to reproduce, allowing colonization of terrestrial environments

**Two major clades within Amniota:**

|Clade|Temporal Fenestrae|Includes|
|---|---|---|
|**Synapsida**|One (lower temporal fenestra)|Mammals + their stem ancestors (_e.g., Dimetrodon_)|
|**Diapsida**|Two (upper + lower temporal fenestra)|Reptiles, dinosaurs, birds|
|**Anapsida**|None|Turtles (uncertain placement — see below)|

- **Synapsids** have historically been called "mammal-like reptiles" — but they are _outside_ Reptilia; they are not reptiles
- Key synapsid trend: **progressive loss of lower jaw bones** over mammalian evolution; the articular and quadrate bones became the **inner ear bones** of modern mammals (the malleus and incus)
- **Diapsids** include everything the rest of this course will focus on: lizards, snakes, crocodilians, dinosaurs, and birds

---
### What Is a Reptile?

- The term _Reptilia_ means "to crawl" — historically defined as scaly, four-legged crawling animals
- Problem: no exclusive shared derived characters define "reptile" — it's not a clean clade
- **Modern definition (phylogenetic):** Reptilia = _Captorhinomorpha_ + _Aves_ + their most recent common ancestor
    - Everything within that monophyletic group = a reptile (including birds!)
- _(Textbook: Box 4.2, p. 78)_

---
### Turtles (Anapsida) — Uncertain Placement

- No temporal fenestrae (openings) behind the eye
- Missing link between proto-turtles and modern turtles = mystery
- Best current hypothesis: turtles are highly derived **diapsids** that secondarily lost their temporal openings (fusing the skull into a robust box)
- Turtle shell = ribs expanded to the outside of the body
- Placement on cladogram currently marked with a **question mark** at the diapsid node

---
### Lepidosauria

Not covered in detail; includes living lizards, snakes, and the **tuatara** (_Sphenodon_)

- Tuatara is endemic to New Zealand and is the sole surviving member of the order Rhynchocephalia
- Had a very diverse Mesozoic lineage; tuatara is the only living survivor

## Part 2: Vertebrate Anatomy & Terminology

Reference: Platiosaurus skeletal diagram (PDF posted in Brightspace module — recommended to keep handy throughout the course)

### General Terms

|Term|Meaning|
|---|---|
|**Cranial**|Of or relating to the skull|
|**Post-cranial**|Everything that is not the skull|
|**Skull**|Everything in front of the 1st cervical vertebra, including upper and lower jaws|
|**Mandible**|The lower jaw|
|**Axial**|Of the midline / vertebral column|
|**Appendicular**|Of the limbs|

---
### Anatomical Directions

|Term|Meaning|Example|
|---|---|---|
|**Dorsal**|Back/top|Spine is dorsal|
|**Ventral**|Front/belly|Belly is ventral|
|**Medial**|Toward the midline|Spine is medial to the ribs|
|**Lateral**|Away from the midline|Hands are lateral to the shoulders|
|**Proximal**|Closer to the body|Shoulder is proximal to the hand|
|**Distal**|Further from the body|Hand is distal to the shoulder|
|**Cranial**|Toward the head||
|**Caudal**|Toward the tail||

_Note: dorsal/ventral refer to quadrupedal posture (dorsal = up, ventral = down), but the same terms apply to bipedal animals._

---
### The Vertebral Column

|Region|Name|
|---|---|
|Neck vertebrae|**Cervical** vertebrae|
|Back vertebrae (shoulder to pelvis)|**Dorsal** vertebrae|
|Pelvis-supporting vertebrae|**Sacral** vertebrae (fuse to form the **sacrum** in derived forms)|
|Tail vertebrae|**Caudal** vertebrae|

**Individual vertebra anatomy:**

- **Centrum** — main body of the vertebra
- **Neural canal** — opening above centrum through which the spinal cord passes
- **Neural spine** — projects upward (the bumps you feel running a finger down your back)
- **Neural arches** — support the ribs

---
### Pectoral Girdle (Forelimb Support)

- **Scapula** — shoulder blade
- **Clavicle** — collarbone (horizontal bone across top of chest; frequently broken)
- **Coracoid** — additional support bone (present in dinosaurs/birds, reduced in mammals)

**Forelimb bones:**

|Bone|Location|
|---|---|
|**Humerus**|Upper arm|
|**Radius**|Lower arm (medial)|
|**Ulna**|Lower arm (lateral)|
|**Carpals**|Wrist bones (small, blocky)|
|**Metacarpals**|Palm bones|
|**Phalanges**|Finger bones; numbered I (medial/thumb) to V (lateral/pinky)|

---
### Pelvic Girdle (Hindlimb Support)

- **Ilium** (pl. _ilia_) — plate-like bone attached to the vertebral column
- **Ischium** (pl. _ischia_) — projects downward/backward
- **Pubis** (pl. _pubes_) — projects downward/forward

_Direction the ischium and pubis point will be used later to distinguish dinosaur groups._

**Hindlimb bones:**

|Bone|Location|
|---|---|
|**Femur**|Upper leg|
|**Tibia**|Lower leg (larger bone)|
|**Fibula**|Lower leg (thinner bone — commonly broken in skiing/skating injuries)|
|**Tarsals**|Ankle bones (includes **astragalus** and **calcaneum** — important for dinosaur identification later)|
|**Metatarsals**|Foot bones|
|**Phalanges**|Toe bones; numbered I (medial) to V (lateral)|

---
### Skull Bones

**Braincase / Skull roof:**

- **Frontal** — front of braincase
- **Parietal** — rear of braincase
- **Squamosal** — lateral side of skull

**Key opening:**

- **Foramen magnum** — large opening at the back/base of the skull through which the spinal cord exits the brain

**Skull-neck joint:**

- **Occipital condyle** — the portion of the skull that articulates with the 1st cervical vertebra, allowing head movement

**Upper jaw (tooth-bearing bones):**

- **Premaxilla** (pl. _premaxillae_) — tip of the snout; paired but often fuse; forms the front of the nostril opening
- **Maxilla** (pl. _maxillae_) — main tooth-bearing bone of the upper jaw
- **Nasals** — bones forming the roof of the nasal passage

**Lower jaw (mandible) in dinosaurs:**

- **Dentary** — main tooth-bearing bone (two in dinosaurs, fused in mammals)
- **Angular, Surangular, Articular** — bones at the back of the jaw; the articular and quadrate become inner ear bones in mammal evolution
