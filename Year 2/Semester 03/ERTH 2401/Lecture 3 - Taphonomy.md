## Taphonomy

**Definition**
The study of how organisms transition from the biosphere (the living world) into the lithosphere (the rock record). The term was coined mid-20th century by Russian geologist **Efremov**. Originally studied to inform paleoecology and behaviour, it has since become a standalone field integrating chemistry, biology, and geology.

#### Key concept: TAZ (Taphonomically Active Zone)
The zone at and below the ground surface where scavengers, microbes, plant roots, and burrowing organisms actively degrade biological material. A bone in the TAZ is being recycled. Burial below the TAZ is a critical step toward fossilization. Deeper burial also produces _diagenetic halos_ — microbial decay can actually encourage mineral precipitation around a bone, aiding preservation.

#### MME — Mass Mortality Events
An acronym increasingly studied today as climate change drives more frequent die-offs. Events where large numbers of animals die together are of major taphonomic and ecological interest. Overwhelming scavenger capacity is key to bone preservation at these sites.

## The "Rules" For Becoming A Fossil

#### Rule 1
**Have hard parts.** Mineralized skeletons (phosphate in vertebrates; calcite, aragonite, silica in invertebrates) dramatically increase preservation odds. Even a fragment can be identifiable. Soft-bodied preservation requires exceptional conditions — rapid burial, microbial mats, or perfect storm chemistry (e.g., Ediacaran fauna).

#### Rule 2
**Get buried quickly — avoid the TAZ.** Rapid burial by sediment protects remains from recyclers. Key burial mechanism: _abruption_ — a sudden large-scale sedimentation event (e.g., volcanic ash fall, turbidite, flood deposit) that buries organisms essentially in place. Classic example: **Ashfall Fossil Beds, Nebraska** — a Yellowstone-related ash fall buried a herd of rhinos ~12 million years ago, many found fully articulated.

#### Rule 3
**Don't die alone — be gregarious.** Herd animals dying in mass mortality events swamp scavenger capacity, leaving bones unrecycled. Bone beds are also far more likely to be discovered by paleontologists — hundreds of bones weathering from a hillside are hard to miss, while a single isolated skeleton may never be found.

#### Rule 4
**Be fossorial (burrow underground).** Animals that habitually live underground are naturally sheltered from surface recycling processes. Example: _Diictodon_ (Permian synapsid, Karoo Basin) found as articulated pairs, likely preserved in their own burrows. Debated dinosaur example: _Oryctodromeus_, a small ornithopod, possibly fossorial.

#### Rule 5
**Live near water in a depositional basin.** Bones in water decay more slowly than bones exposed to air. Tectonic basins receiving large sediment input (e.g., the Western Interior Basin from the Gulf of Mexico to Arctic Canada) are ideal. The basin sinks, sediment accumulates, and fossils are buried deeper rather than re-eroded. Living in a depositional basin is perhaps the single most important factor in the long-term fossil record.

#### Rule 6
**Get buried in the right chemistry.** Burial pH matters. Slightly alkaline environments (e.g., ocean sediments) favor preservation. Acidic environments — swamps, tropical forest soils — dissolve bone. This is why the hominin fossil record is sparse: primates often died in acidic forest settings.

## Transport & the Voorhees groups

Classic work by Michael Voorhees (Univ. of Nebraska) categorized bones into three groups by transport potential in fluid. Heavily referenced but little tested. Recent work by PhD student Michael Chapone at the Univ. of Minnesota's **St. Anthony Falls Lab** used the Mississippi River in a large flume to test this with 3D-printed dinosaur bones and sheep bones. Key findings: the three-group model is oversimplified; bones mostly moved only a few meters before settling into hollows between ripples/dunes. Conclusion: fossils are almost always found in or near the environment the animal actually lived in.

## Case Studies

#### Fighting dinosaurs (Mongolia)
_Velociraptor_ and _Protoceratops_, found locked together. Collected 1971 from the Djadokhta Formation (southern Gobi). Geologist David Loope recognized the enclosing sediment as an **abruption deposit** — the front face of a sand dune detached after rain and buried the animals instantaneously, preserving them mid-action.

#### Dueling dinosaurs (Montana)
Commercially collected specimen; much context information already lost. Dr. Eric Roberts (Colorado School of Mines) is studying it. Rogers skeptical of the "dueling" interpretation — parsimony favors simpler explanations. New radiometric ages for the Hell Creek Formation recently published.

#### Majunga Basin, Madagascar
Long-running project (Rogers joined 1996, 8+ expeditions). Exceptionally rich bone beds with _Majungasaurus_ (theropod), _Rapetosaurus_ (sauropod), crocodilians, birds, frogs, snakes. Notable findings: best evidence for dinosaur cannibalism (tooth marks on _Majungasaurus_ bones); puparial chambers from carrion insects preserved on bones; microbial tunneling visible under SEM. Entire fauna is sauropodomorphs and theropods only — no ornithischians, suggesting Madagascar was isolated before they could colonize.

#### Centrosaurus bone bed (Alberta)
Ryan and Rogers discussed the thorough disarticulation seen in Ceratopsian bone beds in Alberta and Montana. Rogers leans toward a wet-environment explanation (standing water, shallow lake or oxbow) rather than the multi-step flood/exposure/re-flood model. Parsimony favors fewer steps. Both agree further study is warranted.
