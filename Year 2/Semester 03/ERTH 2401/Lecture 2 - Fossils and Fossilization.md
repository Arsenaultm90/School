## What is a fossil?

#### Definition
Preserved remains or traces of ancient animals or plants. Does not include human artifacts (those fall under archaeology). The word likely derives from the Latin _fossilius_ — "dug up." Fossils are generally defined as more than 10,000 years old, the approximate minimum time for fossilization to occur, though the exact duration is not well constrained.

#### How rare are fossils?
Extremely rare — the odds of any one organism becoming a fossil are astronomical (on the order of one in millions). However, because life has been so abundant over Earth's history, fossils do form. Almost everything we know about ancient life comes from them.

## Types of fossilization

#### Replacement
Molecule-by-molecule substitution of the original bone mineral (calcium hydroxyapatite) by other minerals percolating through groundwater (e.g., fluorapatite). The organic bone is converted into rock-like mineral. The deep brown color of Cretaceous fossils in Alberta comes from manganese and iron deposited this way.

#### Permineralization
Minerals from groundwater infill the pores and voids within bones (spaces originally occupied by blood vessels, nerves, capillaries). The bone structure is preserved but solidified. A cross-section of a permineralized bone can reveal growth lines (like tree rings) used to determine an animal's age at death.

## Types of fossils

#### Body fossils
The physical remains of an organism — usually hard tissues (bones, teeth, shells). Can also include preserved soft tissues: keratin (beaks, claws, fingernails), original organic material (the controversial Mary Schweitzer soft tissue from T. rex), feathers, and skin impressions. Notable soft tissue examples include the Liaoning deposits of China — fine-grained limestone lake sediments preserving feathers, retinal tissue, and scales.

#### Trace fossils
Evidence of organism activity, not the body itself. Most common are footprints (molds and casts). Also includes burrows, feeding traces, and coprolites (fossilized feces). From tracks you can infer: the type of animal, its approximate size, speed of movement, metabolic rate, and social behavior (herding, predator-prey interactions). The Ottawa/Gatineau shales contain worm and invertebrate burrow traces from ancient seafloors.

#### Other fossil types
Coprolites — fossilized feces. The T. rex coprolite from Saskatchewan's Frenchman Formation is about 18 inches wide and contains crushed bone fragments and organic muscle tissue. It's attributed to T. rex because that is the only large theropod known from that formation. Gastroliths — stones swallowed to aid digestion, found in stomach regions of fossils. Amber — hardened tree sap that can preserve insects, plants, and occasionally vertebrate tissue in remarkable detail.

## Soft tissue preservation

#### Mummification
Desiccation in dry, arid caves removes moisture and prevents microbial decay. Examples: a 50,000-year-old wolf pup, a 30,000-year-old cave lion cub. Mammoth and mastodon mummies from Siberian peat bogs — low oxygen and high acidity in peat inhibit bacterial decay.

#### Mary Schweitzer's soft tissue
Researcher at North Carolina State, working with Jack Horner. Extracted what appears to be original soft tissue (capillaries with blood cells) from a T. rex femur — potentially unaltered over ~70 million years. Still controversial but supported by multiple lines of chemical and microscopic evidence. More details coming in the paleometabolism lecture.

#### Feather preservation — Liaoning deposits, China
_Sinosauropteryx_ was the first dinosaur with undisputed direct evidence of feathers — a fine black fuzz visible around the body outline, preserved in lithographic limestone. Preservation is so fine that retinal tissue is visible in the skull. Ornithischian dinosaurs also show integumentary structures — early horned dinosaurs like _Psittacosaurus_ have quill-like filaments on the tail. Feather origins are now thought to predate dinosauria, possibly originating in the wider archosaur lineage.

## Where to find fossils — Rock Types

#### Look in sedimentary rocks
Three rock types exist: igneous (volcanic origin), metamorphic (heat/pressure-transformed), and sedimentary (deposited by wind or water). Fossils are almost exclusively found in sedimentary rocks — igneous heat destroys organic material, and metamorphic processes destroy fossils already in the rock. Sedimentary rocks in badlands (Dinosaur Provincial Park, Gobi Desert) are ideal because they are well-exposed with minimal vegetation cover.

#### Reading the rock layers
White sandstones = high-energy, straight river channels (coarse grains require more energy to move). Dark mudstones = low-energy, meandering rivers. Coal layers = compressed ancient plant material. The best places to find fossils are at the color-transition zones between mudstones and sandstones, where energy changes in the water caused bones being carried along to settle and become buried.

## Collecting fossils — field to museum

#### Planning and prospecting
Requires: sedimentary rock of the right age, terrestrial (not marine) deposition, good exposure (little vegetation cover), and correct permits and landowner permissions. There is no technology shortcut — fossil hunting means walking the landscape and recognizing bone shapes by experience. Brown dust from a jackhammer = bone (manganese/iron pigmentation). You learn the "search image" and then find fossils you previously walked past.

#### Excavation and jacketing
Once found, expose lateral margins of the bone. Leave 6–8 inches of supporting matrix around it and dig a pedestal below. Cover with damp toilet paper (separation layer), then apply burlap strips soaked in plaster of Paris. Flip when hardened, remove the pedestal base, and close the jacket. The resulting "field jacket" — a hard white plaster block — protects the fossil for transport. Small fossils get 2–3 layers; large specimens may need up to 25.

#### Preparation and curation
Back at the lab, specialist preparators grind away matrix using fine tools — a process that can take years for large specimens (Mark Mitchell at the ROM spent ~2 years on a single marine reptile). Prepared specimens are catalogued in museum databases, stored in racks/cabinets, and either displayed, replicated in fiberglass, or scanned digitally. Research papers are then written, press releases issued, and public outreach follows. Fossils in Alberta belong to the province; in Mongolia, to the country — researchers are stewards, not owners.

## Fossil dating — sedimentology

#### Numerical (Absolute) Dating - Geochronology
Radiometric dating uses unstable isotopes in minerals that decay at known rates. Best source: volcanic ash layers within sedimentary sequences. Parent isotope → daughter isotope + heat, at a known half-life. For dinosaur paleontology, potassium-argon (K-Ar) decay is primary (half-life: 1.3 billion years). Carbon-14 (half-life: 5,730 years) works for Cenozoic mammals. Uranium-lead used for very deep time. The abbreviation Ma = millions of years ago.

#### Relative Dating - Litho- & Biostratigraphy
More commonly used in practice — expensive radiometric dates aren't always available. Uses lithostratigraphy (rock layer relationships) and biostratigraphy (characteristic fossils tied to specific time windows). Index fossils, species with global distribution and rapid evolution rates, allow correlation of rock ages worldwide. Ammonites are especially useful index fossils for the Mesozoic. A defined time interval characterized by a specific fossil species is called a biozone.

#### Steno's Laws of Stratigraphy
- **Superposition** — Older rocks are deposited first; younger rocks on top. The bottom of any undeformed sequence is older than the top.
- **Original horizontality** — Sediment layers are always deposited flat. Tilted or folded layers were deformed after deposition by tectonic activity.
- **Lateral continuity** — Rock layers extend horizontally in all directions; the same layer on either side of a valley was once continuous.
- **Cross-cutting relationships** — Any feature that cuts through a rock (fault, intrusion, river) is younger than the rock it cuts through.
