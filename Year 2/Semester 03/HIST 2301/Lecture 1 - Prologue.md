
## The Challenge of Governing Canada

Canada presents a unique governing challenge: it is bilingual but not small (like Belgium), vast but not densely populated (unlike the US, Russia, or China), and federal but lacking a single defining unifying moment (no revolution, no dramatic separation). Its internal fractures — while real — have been less violent than comparable divisions in Northern Ireland, Spain, or South Africa. This unique complexity shapes all of its political history.

## The long 19th century (1789–1914)

Term coined by historian Eric Hobsbawm (1917–2012). Deliberately stretches beyond calendar boundaries to capture long-term trends. The period runs from the French Revolution (1789) to the outbreak of WWI (1914). Key value: it emphasizes continuity and persistence over dramatic ruptures.

**The spread of liberal democracy**
The three ideals of the French Revolution — _liberté, égalité, fraternité_ — spread across the Atlantic world during this period. They provided the vocabulary to challenge slavery, oligarchy, and exploitative labor. These ideals became foundational to 19th-century liberalism, which centered the rights of the individual (in practice: the adult male).

**Shifting models of settlement**
Earlier colonial models were corporate and communal: the village commons, the seigneury, the Clergy Reserves. By the late 19th century the homestead emerges as the new model — based on the nuclear family headed by a senior adult male. Authority shifts from lord/manor to household patriarch.

**Liberalism in Canada, 1867–1914**
Loyalty to the monarchy persists, but sovereignty in practice rests with Parliament and the electorate. Every expansion of Canadian territory and policy is framed in the language of individual rights: voters' rights, land rights, religious freedom (Christian only). Conflicts of 1850–1918 are conceptualized as battles for freedom. Importantly, Canadian liberalism adds a specific dimension: freedom _from_ the United States.

## Progress and Modernity

**The idea of progress**
No word better captures the 19th and 20th century spirit than "progress." It carried a belief that the past was largely static and the present/future were domains of positive transformation. Scientific knowledge was increasingly seen as the engine of social improvement. This worldview was embodied by the Whig School of historians, who read all of history as evidence of continuous human advancement.

**What modernity meant**
Modernity = a definitive break with the pre-modern world. Science over superstition; individual merit over inherited class; city over countryside. Authority shifts away from landed gentry and clergy. Contemporary with Darwinian evolutionary theory — not coincidentally.

**Canada as a modern project**
Canada was created in the forges of modernity — its constitution hard-wired the values of its era. The history of Canada since 1867 is, in one reading, the story of an idea: modernity. Over 150 years of media and educational institutions have advanced and reproduced this intellectual context.

## Contradictions of liberalism

**The core contradiction**
Generations that called for individual rights simultaneously denied them to groups categorized as collectivities outside the paradigm of individualism. Individual Aboriginal people, individual Asian immigrants, individual women — all denied individual rights and equality. Moreover: in racializing others, White Canada racialized itself; in classing workers as "other," middle-class Canada defined itself as a class; in essentializing women as unfit for civic life, men essentialized themselves.

**Three fault lines**
1. Race
	Racial categories were treated as commonsensical, scientific, and immutable. A White European (British/French) dominant culture classified and excluded Chinese immigrants, Indigenous peoples, and others. Asian Canadians got the vote in 1948; Indigenous Canadians in 1960.

2. Class
	Industrial working-class collective responses (unions, labor organizations) were viewed by the middle and upper classes as corrosive of individual values — a direct tension with liberal ideology.

3. Gender
	So long as "individual" was defined as male, women's movements calling for social and electoral reform were seen as problematic. Women's suffrage was repeatedly rejected before WWI forced a reconsideration.


**WWI as a turning point**
The Great War challenged and changed modernity's contradictions. Before the war: voluntarism (men chose to serve), women's suffrage rejected, labour demands suppressed. As war continued: women gained the vote, labour-state relations were revisited, and conscription was introduced — forcing Canadian men to fight for their liberty, without apparent irony.

## The short 20th century (1914/1919–1991)

**The concept**
Hobsbawm's companion concept. Runs from WWI (or the Treaty of Versailles) to the fall of the Berlin Wall and Soviet Union in 1991. Characterized by: nation-building, two conflicting ideologies (liberal democracy vs. totalitarianism), the rise of two superpowers after 1945, an expanded welfare state, and a gradual reconsideration of race and inclusion.


**Canada's difficult 20th century**
- WWI era
	Economy in tatters. Racism and xenophobia commonplace. Internment camps for "enemy" populations whose loyalty was assumed to be foreign.

- 1930s
	Internment camps reopened to house unemployed men during the Depression.

- WWII
	Italian, German, and Japanese Canadians had liberties severely curtailed.

- 1948
	Asian Canadians gain the franchise.

- 1960
	Indigenous Canadians gain the franchise.

- Post-WWII
	Prosperity after a long period of hardship. Cities transform from industrial hubs to revitalized metropolitan capitals. Aboriginal peoples increasingly take a leading role in reorienting the country.


**Change and contradiction**
Liberal ideals of individual rights coexisted with systematic exclusions based on race, class, and gender. Every expansion of rights in Canadian history occurs against resistance from those already holding them.

**Continuity**
Despite dramatic changes over 150+ years, certain structures, ideologies, and tensions persist. The Hobsbawm frameworks are useful precisely because they resist over-emphasizing ruptures at the expense of long-run continuities.
