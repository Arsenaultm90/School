The White Paper is fascinating because it backfired so completely. Trudeau and Chrétien (who drafted it as Indian Affairs minister) genuinely believed they were offering liberation — full citizenship, end of the Indian Act, assimilation into Canadian society as equals. Instead it produced the opposite of its intended effect: it galvanized Indigenous political organization at a national scale and forced the federal government into a defensive retreat within two years. Your thesis can essentially be that the White Paper was the catalytic moment that transformed Indigenous political advocacy from fragmented and regional into a coherent national movement.

**A natural essay structure**
- Brief background on the Indian Act and the political context of 1969 — why Trudeau thought this was the right move, his "just society" framing
- What the White Paper actually proposed and why Indigenous leaders read it as a threat rather than an offer
- The immediate political response — Harold Cardinal and the National Indian Brotherhood's "Red Paper" (_Citizens Plus_, 1970)
- The withdrawal of the White Paper in 1971 and what that represented as a political moment
- Longer-term consequences — NIB becoming the Assembly of First Nations, the constitutional conferences of the early 1980s, Section 35 of the Constitution Act

**Your anchor sources to find**
- Harold Cardinal, _The Unjust Society_ (1969) — this is a primary source and essential
- Sally Weaver, _Making Canadian Indian Policy: The Hidden Agenda 1968–70_ (1981) — this is your key scholarly secondary source, basically the definitive account
- Anything by John Tobias on federal Indian policy history
