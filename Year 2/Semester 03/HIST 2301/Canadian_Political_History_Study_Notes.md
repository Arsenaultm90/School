## UNIT 1: HISTORICAL DEMOGRAPHY OF CANADA, 1608–1921

### Key Demographic Milestones

- **1608**: Samuel de Champlain founds Quebec City — the beginning of sustained French colonial settlement
- **New France** grew slowly; the French Crown prioritized the fur trade over large-scale colonization
- Population of New France reached ~70,000 by the time of the British Conquest (1759–1760)
- **1763 Treaty of Paris**: France cedes New France to Britain → French Canadians become a minority under British rule
- **Loyalist influx (post-1783)**: American Revolution drives ~50,000 United Empire Loyalists into British North America, dramatically reshaping the English-speaking population
- **1791 Constitutional Act**: divides Quebec into Upper Canada (anglophone) and Lower Canada (francophone)

### Population Growth Patterns

- High birth rates in Quebec ("revanche des berceaux" — revenge of the cradle) maintained French Canadian population despite immigration of English settlers
- Large waves of British immigration, especially post-Napoleonic Wars (1815 onward)
- Irish immigration surges dramatically after the **Great Famine (1845–1852)**
- By Confederation (1867), Canada's population was roughly 3.5 million
- **1871 first national census** established baseline data for the new Dominion
- Population grew rapidly to ~8 million by 1921, driven by the Laurier-era immigration boom

### Key Demographic Tensions

- French-English demographic balance was a constant political concern
- Immigration policy was never neutral — it shaped who Canada "was"
- Indigenous populations were systematically undercounted and marginalized in census data

## UNIT 2: THE AGE OF FEDERATION

### 2.1 Introduction to Confederation

**Why Confederation?**
- British North American colonies faced several pressures converging in the 1860s:
  - American expansionism (threat of Manifest Destiny, especially post-Civil War)
  - The threat that Britain might reduce its military commitment to the colonies
  - Political deadlock in the Province of Canada (Canada West vs. Canada East required double majorities)
  - Economic necessity — a larger internal market and shared railway costs
- **Charlottetown Conference (1864)**: initial discussions; **Quebec Conference (1864)**: the 72 Resolutions drafted; **London Conference (1866)**: final terms negotiated
- **July 1, 1867**: British North America Act (now the Constitution Act, 1867) creates the Dominion of Canada with four provinces: Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick

### The Federal Structure (Macdonald's Design)

- **Macdonald's vision**: a strong central government — the federal government held residual powers ("Peace, Order, and Good Government"), unlike the US where states held residual powers
- Federal government could disallow provincial legislation (reservation and disallowance)
- Ottawa controlled trade, banking, criminal law, Indigenous affairs
- Provinces controlled education, property/civil rights, local matters
- **Senate** designed as a check on the elected Commons, appointed regionally
- **Compromise from the start**: Quebec insisted on protected language and civil law rights; Nova Scotia and New Brunswick were lukewarm

Federal government holds residual powers through:
**Peace, Order and Good Government (POGG)**

Confederation was a compromise.
Quebec demanded:
- Protection of French language
- Protection of civil law

Maritime colonies worried about domination by central Canada.

### 2.2 Nova Scotia's Second Thoughts

- Nova Scotia had strong ties to the maritime trade economy and felt Confederation served central Canadian interests more than theirs
- **Joseph Howe** led the anti-Confederation movement in Nova Scotia; feared economic domination by central Canada
- In the 1867 federal election, anti-Confederation candidates swept Nova Scotia
- Nova Scotia sent a delegation to London seeking repeal — it was refused
- Howe eventually negotiated **"Better Terms"** (improved financial arrangements) and accepted Confederation reluctantly, joining the federal cabinet in 1869
- **Lesson**: Confederation was not universally celebrated; it required ongoing management of regional grievances — a theme that never went away

Most important anti-Confederate leader:
Joseph Howe
	Arguments:
	- Confederation would weaken Nova Scotia
	- Ottawa would dominate the region

### 2.3 British Columbia and the Terms of Union

- BC was a British colony facing debt, declining gold rush revenues, and American pressure (Oregon boundary had already been lost in 1846)
- Joined Canada in **1871** on the promise of:
  - A transcontinental railway within 10 years
  - Assumption of BC's colonial debt
- The railway promise would become a massive political issue (see the Pacific Scandal, 1873)
- BC's entry was driven by geography and economics — it needed a connection to the rest of Canada, and feared US annexation

##### Why BC Considered Confederation
British Columbia faced:
- Debt
- End of gold rush prosperity
- Geographic isolation
- Fear of American expansion

British Columbia joined largely because of:
- Transportation
- Economic survival
- Fear of annexation


### 2.4 Prince Edward Island

- PEI rejected Confederation in 1867 — its concerns centred on the land question (absentee landlords controlled most of the island) and feeling its interests would be ignored
- Joined Canada in **1873** after negotiating terms including:
  - Federal government to buy out the absentee landlords
  - Year-round communication with the mainland (ferry service)
- PEI's entry shows that the promise of federal resources could overcome local resistance

##### Why PEI Rejected Confederation
Main concern:
	Land Question
	Much island land belonged to absentee landlords.
	Island residents wanted this solved first.
	They also feared:
	- Being ignored in Ottawa

##### Why PEI Joined (1873)
Canada offered:
1. Buyout of absentee landlords
2. Transportation links

Year-round communication with mainland Canada.

##### Significance
Federal resources convinced PEI to join.

### 2.5 Canada Captures the West, 1867

- At Confederation, the vast Northwest (Rupert's Land) was owned by the Hudson's Bay Company
- Canada purchased Rupert's Land in **1869–1870** for £300,000 — a transaction made without consulting the inhabitants (Métis, First Nations, and some settlers already living there)
- The Red River Settlement (present-day Manitoba) had a significant Métis population led by **Louis Riel**
- **Red River Resistance (1869–1870)**: Métis blocked the survey teams and Canadian government officials, established a Provisional Government, and negotiated the terms of Manitoba's entry into Confederation
- **Manitoba Act, 1870**: Manitoba enters Confederation with bilingual guarantees and Métis land rights (Section 31 — 1.4 million acres promised) — these rights were systematically undermined in practice
- **Northwest Territories Act, 1870**: rest of the land becomes a federal territory under Ottawa's control

The government promised Métis land protections.
In practice many promises were not fulfilled.
This becomes a major cause of the 1885 resistance.

### 2.6 Canada and the First Nations of the West

- The federal government signed a series of **Numbered Treaties (1871–1921)** with First Nations across the prairies and beyond
- Treaties 1–7 covered the southern prairies; Indigenous peoples agreed to cede land in exchange for reserves, annual payments, and promises of education and tools for agriculture
- From a Canadian government perspective: acquiring land for settlement; from an Indigenous perspective: many believed they were agreeing to share the land, not permanently give it up — a fundamental misunderstanding of terms
- **Indian Act, 1876**: consolidated earlier legislation into a comprehensive system of federal control over "Status Indians"
  - Defined who was "Indian" (and who lost status)
  - Controlled band governance, land sales, movement, and cultural practices
  - Banned the potlatch and sun dance ceremonies (1885 amendment)
  - Pass system (informal but pervasive): required Indigenous people to get a pass from an Indian agent to leave the reserve
- The buffalo herds were nearly exterminated by the 1880s, destroying the economic base of prairie Indigenous nations and making them dependent on government rations

##### Indian Act (1876)
Possibly the most important legislation in Indigenous history.

Purpose:
Federal control of Indigenous peoples.
##### What It Did
Defined:
- Status Indian

Controlled:
- Band government
- Reserve land
- Movement
- Cultural practices


### 2.7 Rebellion 1885

**Background:**
- By 1885, the Métis had been pushed further west after the Red River situation; many resettled in the Saskatchewan country
- Their land claims were again being threatened by the incoming surveyors and settlers
- Gabriel Dumont and the Métis appealed to the federal government repeatedly — they were ignored
- Louis Riel was brought back from Montana to lead a political movement

**The North-West Resistance:**
- March 1885: Riel declared a Provisional Government again; armed resistance at **Duck Lake** (Métis victory)
- Major engagements: **Batoche** (May 9–12) — federal forces under General Middleton defeated the Métis
- Riel surrendered; Dumont fled to the US

**Indigenous Involvement:**
- Cree chiefs **Big Bear** and **Poundmaker** were caught up in the resistance; some of their followers attacked settlements (Frog Lake Massacre)
- Both were convicted of treason-felony; the fate of the plains Cree was effectively sealed

### 2.8 Making Sense of 1885

**The Trial and Hanging of Louis Riel:**
- Riel was tried for high treason in Regina (a court with no French-speaking jurors)
- His lawyers tried an insanity defence; Riel rejected it — he wanted to make a political statement
- Found guilty and hanged on **November 16, 1885**
- The execution became one of the most divisive events in Canadian history:
  - **English Canada**: largely supported the hanging; Riel seen as a rebel and murderer
  - **French Canada**: Riel was a martyr — a French Catholic hero betrayed by an English Protestant government
  - **Macdonald's famous line**: "He shall hang though every dog in Quebec bark in his favour"
- The hanging alienated Quebec from the Conservative Party for a generation, accelerating the rise of Wilfrid Laurier and the Liberals

**Legacy of 1885:**
- The CPR (completed just months later, November 1885) was used to move troops west — the railway proved its strategic value immediately
- The resistance ended meaningful Métis land claims for decades
- Riel was officially rehabilitated by a Parliament resolution in 1992
- 1885 is a lens for understanding the competing visions of Canada: Indigenous rights vs. settler colonialism, French vs. English tensions, federal power vs. regional resistance
  

1. Confederation occurred on **July 1, 1867**.
2. The **British North America Act** created Canada.
3. Macdonald wanted a **strong central government**.
4. **Joseph Howe** led anti-Confederation efforts in Nova Scotia.
5. British Columbia joined in **1871** because of the railway promise.
6. PEI joined in **1873** after negotiations over the land question.
7. Canada purchased **Rupert's Land** from the Hudson's Bay Company in **1869-70**.
8. **Louis Riel** led both the Red River Resistance and the North-West Resistance.
9. The **Indian Act (1876)** established extensive federal control over Indigenous peoples.
10. Riel was executed on **November 16, 1885**, creating a major divide between English and French Canada.

### One-Sentence Summary of Unit 2
**The Age of Federation was Canada's attempt to build a nation through Confederation, western expansion, railways, and federal power, but it simultaneously created lasting conflicts involving regional alienation, French-English relations, Métis rights, and Indigenous sovereignty.**

## UNIT 3: THE NATIONAL POLICY AND EARLY REFORM MOVEMENTS

### 3.3 The National Policy (1879)

**Context:**
- After the Pacific Scandal (1873) brought down Macdonald, the Liberals under Alexander Mackenzie governed until 1878
- Canada was in a prolonged economic depression (global downturn following 1873 financial crisis)
- Macdonald returned to power in 1878, campaigning on the National Policy

**Three Pillars:**
1. **Protective Tariffs**: High tariffs on imported manufactured goods to protect Canadian industry (especially Ontario manufacturers) — rates raised to ~25–35%
2. **Transcontinental Railway**: Complete the Canadian Pacific Railway to connect the country and open the West — the CPR was completed in 1885
3. **Western Settlement**: Populate the prairies to create a market for eastern manufactured goods and to provide grain for export

**Effects:**
- Stimulated industrial growth in Ontario and Quebec
- Created a "National Dream" narrative of coast-to-coast nationhood
- Benefited central Canada at the expense of the West and Maritimes (who paid higher prices for manufactured goods but could not apply reciprocal protection to their exports)
- Set the template for Canadian economic policy for decades — high tariffs remained contentious through to 1988 (Free Trade)

**Alexander Mackenzie's Contributions (1873–1878):**
- Though he lacked Macdonald's grand vision, Mackenzie achieved significant institution-building:
  - Created the **Supreme Court of Canada** (1875)
  - Established the **Royal Military College** (Kingston, 1876)
  - **Dominion Elections Act** — introduced the secret ballot
  - Created the **Auditor General's office**
  - Post Office established as a federal institution
  - Committed to railway building but at a slower, more fiscally conservative pace

The National Policy helped create a transcontinental Canadian economy but disproportionately benefited Ontario and Quebec while contributing to western and maritime grievances.

### 3.8 Early Women's Movement(s) in Canada

**Context:**
- In 19th-century Canada, women could not vote federally, had limited property rights (Married Women's Property Acts came gradually), and were largely excluded from higher education and professions
- The movement had multiple and sometimes overlapping strands

**Temperance and Moral Reform:**
- Many women organized through the **Women's Christian Temperance Union (WCTU)**, founded in Canada in 1874
- Argued that alcohol was the root cause of male violence against women and children, poverty, and family breakdown
- Temperance work naturally led to demands for women's suffrage — women needed the vote to protect their homes
- "Social Purity" campaigns linked to imperialism, eugenics, and Anglo-Protestant assumptions about civilization

**Suffrage Movement:**
- **Nellie McClung** (Manitoba): suffrage activist, author, politician — helped win the vote for Manitoba women in 1916
- **Emily Murphy**: first female magistrate in the British Empire; central to the **Persons Case**
- **Famous Five**: Emily Murphy, Nellie McClung, Irene Parlby, Louise McKinney, Henrietta Muir Edwards — petitioned the Supreme Court asking if women were "persons" under the BNA Act (they had been excluded from the Senate)
  - SCC ruled women were NOT persons (1928)
  - The Judicial Committee of the Privy Council (in London) overturned this in **1929** — women were persons
- **Provincial suffrage milestones**: Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Alberta (1916); Ontario, BC (1917); Federal vote for (some) women: **1918** (extended to most women; women of colour largely excluded)
- Indigenous women did not receive full voting rights federally until **1960**

**Class and Race Dimensions:**
- The mainstream suffrage movement was largely middle-class and white
- It often used racist arguments ("Why can a Chinese man vote and I cannot?")
- Working-class women's concerns (wages, safety, hours) were addressed more by labour organizations than mainstream suffrage groups
- First-wave feminism was deeply entangled with imperialism and the "civilizing mission"

##### Famous Five
The five women were:
- Emily Murphy
- Nellie McClung
- Irene Parlby
- Louise McKinney
- Henrietta Muir Edwards

### 3.9 The Great War and the General Strike

*(See also Unit 6 for more detail on WWI)*

**Labour Radicalism Post-WWI:**
- Soldiers returned from war to find unemployment; workers who had suffered wartime inflation and poor wages demanded change
- International revolutionary politics (Russian Revolution, 1917) inspired Canadian radicals
- **One Big Union (OBU)**: formed in 1919, based in the West, advocated industrial unionism (all workers in one union regardless of trade)

**The Winnipeg General Strike (1919):**
- May–June 1919: ~35,000 workers walked off the job in Winnipeg — one of the largest strikes in Canadian history
- Began in the metal trades and building trades; sympathy strikes spread across the city
- Police, firefighters, postal workers joined; essential services nearly halted
- **"Citizens' Committee of 1000"** organized business opposition
- Federal government intervened: amended the Immigration Act to allow deportation of British-born "alien" agitators
- Mounted police charged a crowd on **"Bloody Saturday" (June 21, 1919)**: 2 killed, many injured, strike leaders arrested
- Strike leaders (including J.S. Woodsworth, later founder of the CCF) faced sedition charges
- **Result**: strike collapsed; workers did not win recognition; but it galvanized the labour movement and planted seeds for progressive political parties


Canada's National Policy used tariffs, railways, and western settlement to build a national economy, but it also deepened regional inequalities. At the same time, women organized through temperance and suffrage movements to gain political rights, culminating in the Persons Case and expanded voting rights. After World War I, workers challenged economic inequality through labour activism and the Winnipeg General Strike, laying the foundation for future labour and social democratic politics.

8. Winnipeg General Strike occurred in **1919**, ending with **Bloody Saturday**.

## UNIT 4: THE LAURIER ERA

### 4.4 The Sunny Ways of Sir Wilfrid Laurier

**Who Was Laurier?**
- First French Canadian Prime Minister (1896–1911)
- Liberal; a master of compromise and conciliation ("sunny ways")
- Governed during a period of great economic growth (the "Laurier Boom")
- Famously declared: "The 20th century belongs to Canada"

**His Political Philosophy:**
- Moderation; compromise between French and English Canada; between imperialism and nationalism
- Believed Canada could best serve its own interests through incremental autonomy within the Empire
- Opposed extreme positions on both sides — angered both ardent imperialists and ardent nationalists

**Economic Record:**
- Presided over massive immigration (see Unit 5)
- Railway expansion: the CPR was the only transcontinental; Laurier added two more (**Grand Trunk Pacific** and **Canadian Northern**) — these would bankrupt and require federal nationalization post-WWI (eventually becoming **CN Rail**)
- Industrial production boomed, partly as a result of Macdonald's National Policy taking hold

### 4.5 Imperialism vs. Nationalism

**The Central Tension of the Laurier Years:**
- **Imperialists** (mainly English Canadian Conservatives): wanted Canada to contribute men and money directly to British imperial ventures; saw the British Empire as Canada's highest loyalty
- **Nationalists** (mainly French Canadian, led by **Henri Bourassa**): wanted Canada to pursue its own interests; deeply suspicious of British wars
- Laurier tried to navigate between these poles — which meant he frequently satisfied neither side

**Naval Question (Laurier's Naval Service Act, 1910):**
- Britain faced German naval challenge; demanded Canadian contribution to the Royal Navy
- Conservatives (Borden) wanted to give Britain $35 million immediately for British battleships
- Laurier proposed a **Canadian naval force** — neither fully British nor fully autonomous
- Nationalists under Bourassa attacked Laurier for any involvement; Conservatives attacked him for not doing enough
- The Naval Service Act contributed to Laurier's 1911 defeat

### 4.6 Canada and Africa (The Boer War)

**The South African (Boer) War (1899–1902):**
- Britain fought Afrikaner settlers (Boers) for control of South Africa — imperialist war
- English Canadian public opinion, press, and Conservative politicians demanded Canada contribute troops
- French Canadian opinion (and Henri Bourassa) strongly opposed — "Why should French Canadians die for British imperialism?"
- Laurier's compromise: the **government would equip volunteers** but would not formally commit to the war as a national obligation
- About 8,000 Canadians served voluntarily; several killed
- Bourassa broke with Laurier over this issue — the beginning of the French Canadian nationalist critique of Laurier
- The Boer War set a precedent: Canada could be expected to contribute militarily to imperial conflicts — this would be tested catastrophically in WWI

### 4.7 Edwardian Crises

**Multiple flashpoints tested Laurier's balancing act:**

**Manitoba Schools Question (pre-1896, resolved by Laurier):**
- Manitoba had eliminated its publicly-funded Catholic schools in 1890
- French Catholics (and the Pope) demanded the federal government use its "remedial power" to restore denominational schools
- Laurier refused to use federal disallowance — negotiated the **Laurier-Greenway compromise** (1896): French could be taught where numbers warranted; Catholic religious instruction allowed after hours — largely a failure for Catholics, but avoided a direct confrontation

**Alaska Boundary Dispute (1903):**
- Canada and the US disputed the Alaska Panhandle boundary
- An international tribunal ruled in favour of the US; the British member sided with the Americans
- Caused fury in Canada — Britain had sacrificed Canadian interests to maintain good relations with the US
- Accelerated Canadian autonomy sentiment: Canada needed to manage its own foreign affairs

**Immigration Controversies:**
- **Anti-Asian racism**: Continuous Journey Regulation (1908) effectively excluded South Asian immigrants; head taxes on Chinese immigrants ($500 by 1903); Gentlemen's Agreement with Japan limited Japanese immigration
- **Komagata Maru (1914)**: A ship carrying 376 South Asian passengers was denied entry to Vancouver — tested and confirmed discriminatory policies

**Reciprocity Agreement (1911):**
- Laurier negotiated a free trade deal with the US (reciprocity)
- English Canadian business and Conservative interests (aligned with the "No Truck or Trade with the Yankees" slogan) opposed it — feared annexation
- Together with the Naval issue, this brought down Laurier in the 1911 election (won by Borden's Conservatives)


# The Whole Laurier Era in One Paragraph

Laurier presided over a period of tremendous economic growth, immigration, and railway expansion, but his government was constantly challenged by the conflict between imperialism and nationalism. Through compromises on the Boer War, the Naval Service Act, and the Manitoba Schools Question, he attempted to balance English and French Canadian interests while gradually increasing Canadian autonomy. Events such as the Alaska Boundary Dispute strengthened Canadian nationalism, while controversies over immigration revealed the racial exclusions of the period. Ultimately, disputes over reciprocity and naval policy led to Laurier's defeat in 1911.

1. Laurier was Canada's **first French Canadian Prime Minister**.
2. The Laurier era is associated with the **Laurier Boom**.
3. The central issue was **Imperialism vs Nationalism**.
4. The **Boer War** was Laurier's first major imperial crisis.
5. The **Naval Service Act (1910)** created a Canadian navy.
6. **Reciprocity (1911)** and the Naval Question contributed to Laurier's defeat.

## UNIT 5: IMMIGRATION AND THE NATIONAL POLICY

### 5.2 Immigration and the National Policy

- The third pillar of the National Policy was Western settlement — immigration was the tool
- Without settlers, the West had no population to buy eastern manufactured goods or grow grain for export
- Before 1896, western settlement was painfully slow — the land was hard, the climate harsh, the railway distances enormous

### 5.3 Immigrants by the Numbers

- 1897–1914: over 3 million immigrants arrived in Canada
- The prairies (Manitoba, Saskatchewan — created 1905, Alberta — created 1905) transformed from virtual wilderness to farmland
- **Ethnic composition** shifted: before 1896, mainly British and American immigrants; after, large numbers of Ukrainians, Poles, Galicians, Germans, Mennonites, Doukhobors, and others from Eastern and Southern Europe

### 5.4 The Clifford Sifton Years, 1896–1905

**Clifford Sifton** as Minister of the Interior under Laurier aggressively promoted immigration:
- Set up offices across Europe; paid bonuses to booking agents for each immigrant who arrived
- Famous quote describing ideal immigrant: "a stalwart peasant in a sheepskin coat, born on the soil, whose forefathers have been farmers for ten generations, with a stout wife and a half-dozen children"
- Prioritized agricultural ability over ethnicity (to a degree — still excluded non-whites)
- Advertised Canada's prairies as the "Last Best West" — the frontier was closing in the US; Canada had cheap land

**Controversy:**
- "Preferred" immigrants: British and American
- "Tolerated" immigrants: Northern and Eastern Europeans
- "Excluded" (officially or effectively): Asians, Blacks, Southern Europeans, Jews

### 5.5 The Promised Land

**The Reality of Prairie Settlement:**
- Hard, isolated homestead life (160 acres under the Dominion Lands Act for a $10 fee, conditional on breaking land and living there 3 of 5 years)
- Harsh climate; crop failures; debt to railways and grain elevator companies
- However, the price of wheat rose in the early 20th century — good years brought genuine prosperity
- Ethnic bloc settlements: Ukrainian bloc in Manitoba/Saskatchewan; Mennonite settlements; Doukhobors in Saskatchewan
- **Impact on Indigenous peoples**: their lands were the ones being given to homesteaders; reserves were shrunk; traditional hunting/gathering lifestyles became impossible

**Residential Schools:**
- Federal government (with the churches — Anglican, Catholic, United, Presbyterian) operated schools across the country, most heavily in the West
- Purpose: "kill the Indian in the child" (attributed to Duncan Campbell Scott, Deputy Superintendent of Indian Affairs)
- Children taken from families, forbidden to speak their languages, subjected to cultural erasure
- Widespread physical and sexual abuse; high death rates from disease (especially tuberculosis)
- The **Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2015)** documented the system as **cultural genocide**


# The Entire Unit in One Paragraph
To make the National Policy succeed, Canada needed settlers for the Prairies. Under Clifford Sifton, millions of immigrants—particularly farmers from Eastern Europe—were recruited between 1897 and 1914 and encouraged to settle on homesteads. Their arrival transformed the Prairies into a major agricultural region and led to the creation of Alberta and Saskatchewan. However, this settlement occurred on Indigenous lands, contributing to the loss of traditional ways of life and supporting policies of assimilation such as the residential school system.

1. Immigration was the **third pillar of the National Policy**.
2. Over **3 million immigrants** arrived between **1897 and 1914**.
3. Clifford Sifton directed the immigration campaign.
4. Sifton wanted **experienced agricultural settlers** ("stalwart peasant").
5. Alberta and Saskatchewan were created in **1905**.
6. Homesteaders could receive **160 acres for $10** under the Dominion Lands Act.
7. Settlement occurred largely on lands taken from Indigenous peoples.
8. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada (2015) described residential schools as **cultural genocide**.

- Railway → Connect Canada

## UNIT 6: THE GREAT WAR, INTERWAR YEARS, AND WORLD WAR II

### 6.2 Borden vs. Borden

- **Robert Borden** (Conservative PM, 1911–1920) faced a different Canada than Laurier
- His 1917 election: the **Union Government** — a coalition of Conservatives and pro-conscription Liberals, specifically formed to pass conscription
- Borden believed Canada's war effort required conscription — but this was deeply divisive along French-English lines
- Borden represented English Canadian imperialist sentiment; he clashed with Quebec's resistance

### 6.3 The Great War (World War I, 1914–1918)

**Canada's Entry:**
- As a Dominion, when Britain declared war (August 4, 1914), Canada was automatically at war — no separate declaration required
- **Sam Hughes** (Minister of Militia): energetically mobilized Canada; also chaotic and self-aggrandizing; eventually fired
- **Canadian Expeditionary Force (CEF)**: over 600,000 Canadians served

**Major Engagements:**
- **Second Battle of Ypres (1915)**: First major Canadian engagement; Germans used poison gas (chlorine); Canadians held the line
- **Battle of the Somme (1916)**: Massive casualties
- **Battle of Vimy Ridge (April 9–12, 1917)**: All four Canadian divisions attacked together for the first time; captured the ridge (which the French and British had failed to take); became a defining national myth — "the birth of a nation" narrative
- **Passchendaele (1917)**: Brutal, costly campaign in the mud
- **Hundred Days Offensive (1918)**: Canada played a leading role in the final Allied offensive that ended the war

**The Home Front:**
- **War Measures Act (1914)**: Gave the federal government sweeping emergency powers — could intern "enemy aliens," censor press, restrict civil liberties
- ~88,000 "enemy aliens" (mostly Ukrainian-Canadians) were interned or forced to report regularly
- Women moved into industrial work, farming, nursing — demonstrated their capacity
- **War bonds, rationing, industrial mobilization**: the state expanded dramatically

### 6.4 Assessing Canada's War

**Positives:**
- Canada gained an independent voice on the world stage — Borden insisted on it
- Imperial War Cabinet participation; separate Canadian signature on the **Treaty of Versailles (1919)**
- Led to the **Statute of Westminster (1931)**: full legislative independence for Dominions
- National pride; sense of distinct Canadian identity

**Negatives:**
- ~61,000 Canadians killed; ~172,000 wounded
- **Conscription Crisis** (1917): tore the country along French-English lines
- "Enemy alien" internment — a betrayal of immigrants
- The trauma of industrial warfare was enormous; veterans returned to a country that did not always appreciate or support them

### 6.5 Suffrage and Prohibition

**Suffrage:**
- **Military Voters Act and Wartime Elections Act (1917)**: Borden gave the vote to women relatives of servicemen and took it away from "enemy aliens" — a nakedly political manipulation of the franchise to ensure a conscription-supporting electorate
- **1918**: All female British subjects over 21 could vote federally (major exceptions: Indigenous women, Asian women)
- Provinces: Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Alberta (1916); Ontario, BC (1917); Quebec (women's provincial vote only in **1940**)

**Prohibition:**
- Provincial prohibition passed across Canada during WWI (pressure from temperance movement + wartime morality)
- Manitoba (1916), Ontario (1916), most other provinces
- Federal wartime prohibition of import/export of liquor (1918)
- Quickly repealed in most provinces post-war: BC (1921), Manitoba (1923), etc. — replaced by government-controlled liquor boards
- Quebec mostly resisted prohibition from the start
- **Lessons**: moral reform movements could achieve legislative wins, but enforcement was weak and public support eroded quickly

### 6.6 The Interwar Years

**Roaring Twenties:**
- Post-war economic boom; consumer society; radio, automobiles, movies
- Women's fashion and behaviour changed (flappers)
- Continued immigration; urbanization
- **But**: labour unrest (Winnipeg 1919), regional grievances, farm discontent

**Wall Street Crash (October 1929):**
- Over-speculation in stocks led to a catastrophic market collapse
- Global credit froze; demand collapsed; trade plummeted
- Canada was especially vulnerable: primary commodity exporter (wheat prices collapsed)

**The Great Depression (1929–1939):**
- Unemployment reached ~27% nationally; far higher in some regions
- Prairie farmers hit doubly hard: depression prices AND the **Dust Bowl** (prolonged drought, grasshoppers — crops failed)
- **Bennett Buggies**: horses pulling cars (no money for gas) — named mockingly after PM R.B. Bennett
- Relief camps for single unemployed men — federal government organized work camps paying 20 cents/day → deeply resented
- **On-to-Ottawa Trek (1935)**: relief camp workers rode freight trains toward Ottawa to protest; stopped in Regina by the RCMP (**Regina Riot**)
- The Depression exposed the limits of the existing federal-provincial division of social responsibilities — provinces were responsible for relief but were bankrupt

### 6.7 The Natural Governing Party: The King Years

**William Lyon Mackenzie King (PM 1921–1926, 1926–1930, 1935–1948)**
- Longest-serving PM in Canadian history
- Master of ambiguity and delay — "Parliament will decide"
- **King-Byng Affair (1926)**: Governor General Lord Byng refused King's request to dissolve Parliament; gave power to Meighen's Conservatives; Meighen lost a confidence vote; election called; King won on the principle of constitutional propriety
  - Established that the Governor General must follow PM's advice in constitutional conventions
  - Led directly to the **Statute of Westminster (1931)**

**King and the Depression:**
- Initially refused to give "a five-cent piece" to provincial Conservative governments for relief (infamously callous statement)
- Lost 1930 election to R.B. Bennett as a result

**R.B. Bennett (1930–1935):**
- Initially raised tariffs even higher (worsened the Depression by reducing trade)
- **Bennett's New Deal (January 1935)**: Inspired by FDR, proposed unemployment insurance, minimum wage, agricultural support — came too late and was declared largely unconstitutional
- King returned to power in 1935

**Third Parties Emerge:**
- **Co-operative Commonwealth Federation (CCF, 1932)**: Founded in Calgary; socialist; wanted state ownership of key industries, social programs — direct response to the Depression; predecessor to the NDP
- **Social Credit (1935)**: Alberta; Major Douglas's monetary theory; argued that banks and credit were the problem; Premier William Aberhart; won Alberta government 1935
- **Union Nationale (Quebec)**: Maurice Duplessis; conservative nationalism; dominated Quebec politics
- Canada was no longer a two-party state — agrarian and labour movements (United Farmers of Ontario, Alberta, etc.) had already established this trend from the late 1910s

### 6.10 Canada Goes to War (World War II)

**Canada's Entry:**
- Canada declared war **September 10, 1939** — a week after Britain, to assert its autonomy (King insisted on the parliamentary debate)
- King had carefully managed public opinion; promised no conscription for overseas service

**Canada's Contribution:**
- ~1.1 million Canadians served (10% of the population)
- Supplied grain, munitions, vehicles, aircraft under the **British Commonwealth Air Training Plan (BCATP)** — Canada trained 131,000 Allied airmen ("the aerodrome of democracy")
- Industrial mobilization: Eldorado uranium (atomic bomb materials), shipbuilding, automotive plants converted
- **Dieppe Raid (August 1942)**: Disastrous; ~6,000 men (mostly Canadians); nearly 60% casualties; tactical intelligence failure
- **Sicily and Italy (1943–1945)**: Canadians served in Mediterranean theater
- **D-Day, June 6, 1944**: Canadians landed at Juno Beach; fought through Normandy
- Liberation of the Netherlands (1944–45): A special bond — Canadians liberated the Dutch; still commemorated

### 6.11 Newfoundland Goes to War

- Newfoundland was NOT part of Canada in WWII (it only joined in 1949)
- Had been a self-governing Dominion but its government went bankrupt during the Depression (1934) — placed under a **Commission of Government** (appointed by Britain)
- Strategically vital: controlled the North Atlantic sea lanes; Gander air base crucial for transatlantic flights
- **Joey Smallwood** led the campaign for Confederation with Canada — narrow referendum victory in 1948 (52.3%)
- Newfoundland's entry in 1949 completed the map of Canada

### 6.12 Status Indians and Military Service in the World Wars

- Indigenous people served in both World Wars in large numbers — disproportionately high relative to their population
- Recognized as skilled hunters and marksmen; served as snipers, messengers, soldiers
- Yet faced discrimination: some initially rejected by recruiters; paid less in some cases; denied veterans benefits after the wars
- Land was taken from reserves while men were overseas
- Voting rights not guaranteed federally until 1960
- Military service both exposed Indigenous soldiers to the outside world AND reinforced demands for better treatment — contributed to post-WWII Indigenous political organizing

### 6.13 Canada between the UK and the US

- WWI accelerated the loosening of imperial ties; WWII completed the shift
- **Ogdensburg Agreement (1940)**: Canada and US established the **Permanent Joint Board on Defence** — the first formal continental defence partnership (while Britain was in crisis); King presented this as protecting Canada, but it marked the shift toward US alignment
- **Hyde Park Declaration (1941)**: Economic integration — US would purchase Canadian war materials, solving Canada's dollar exchange problem
- Post-WWII: UK was bankrupt and diminished; US emerged as the dominant Western power
- Canada's cultural, economic, and military ties pivoted decisively toward the US — with growing ambivalence about this dependence

### 6.14 Global War

- WWII was genuinely global: Pacific, Atlantic, Mediterranean, Eastern Front, North Africa
- Canada served in most theaters but the **European theater** was the primary commitment
- The **Battle of the Atlantic**: German U-boats threatened to sever supply lines; Royal Canadian Navy (RCN) played a major role in convoy escort — arguably one of Canada's most important contributions
- The **Holocaust**: Canada was aware of Nazi persecution of Jews but maintained restrictive immigration policies throughout the war
  - Canada admitted fewer Jewish refugees than almost any other Western nation
  - **None is Too Many**: famous phrase attributed to a Canadian immigration official when asked how many Jewish refugees Canada would accept — encapsulated the government's shameful policy
  - Post-war: guilt about this record influenced Canadian immigration and human rights policy

### 6.15 The Home Front (WWII)

- **National Selective Service**: Directed labour to war industries; women recruited
- Women served in industry (munitions, shipbuilding), replaced men in many fields
- But were expected to return to home roles after the war — and mostly did
- **Rationing and price controls**: federal government managed the economy (gasoline, sugar, butter, meat)
- The federal government took over most taxation from provinces (wartime fiscal centralization)
- **Wartime Information Board**: propaganda and censorship
- **National Resources Mobilization Act (NRMA, 1940)**: conscription for HOME DEFENCE only — a compromise that satisfied almost nobody

### 6.16 Enlisted Women, Conscription, and the Zombie Army

**Women in the Military:**
- Women's divisions created in all three services: **CWAC** (Army), **RCAF-WD** (Air Force), **WRCNS** "Wrens" (Navy)
- ~50,000 women served; not in combat roles
- Paid less than men (2/3 of male pay for most of the war)
- Controversial: were women unfeminine for joining? Were they sexually promiscuous? These anxieties plagued recruitment and public reception

**Conscription Crisis:**
- King's promise: no overseas conscription; NRMA conscripts would stay in Canada
- NRMA conscripts were mockingly called **"Zombies"** by voluntary servicemen — those who fought overseas resented them
- **Conscription Plebiscite (1942)**: King asked Canadians to release him from his anti-conscription promise
  - English Canada voted ~80% YES; Quebec voted ~72% NO — a stark illustration of the national divide
  - King's response: "Not necessarily conscription, but conscription if necessary"
  - Eventually in November 1944, with manpower shortages critical, King ordered 16,000 NRMA men sent overseas — only ~2,500 actually reached the front
- King managed to keep the country together (barely) — his political skill was in delay and ambiguity

### 6.17 Japanese Canadians in the Second World War

- After Pearl Harbor (December 7, 1941), anti-Japanese hysteria swept the BC coast
- **February 1942**: Order-in-Council authorized the removal of all persons of Japanese origin from a 100-mile "protected area" on the BC coast
- ~22,000 Japanese Canadians uprooted: sent to interior camps (Slocan, Greenwood, etc.); property confiscated and sold (often at a fraction of value)
- Two-thirds were **Canadian-born citizens** — citizenship meant nothing
- Families were sometimes separated (men to work camps, women and children to "relocation centres")
- The RCMP and military intelligence found NO evidence of espionage or disloyalty — the policy was driven by racism and the economic interests of those who wanted Japanese Canadian land, fishing licences, and businesses
- Not until **1988** did the federal government formally apologize and pay $21,000 compensation to survivors

## UNIT 7: SOCIAL GOSPEL, TEMPERANCE, EUGENICS, AND REFORM POLITICS

### 7.6 Social Gospel

- A Protestant religious movement that applied Christian ethics to social problems (poverty, inequality, poor working conditions)
- Argued that the Kingdom of God could be built on earth through social reform
- Inspired many progressive reformers; linked to temperance, women's suffrage, labour reform
- Key figures: **J.S. Woodsworth** (Methodist minister, social worker, later CCF founder)
- The social gospel linked religion and politics in Canada's progressive tradition — different from the American religious right; this was a left-leaning Christianity
- Faded as formal theology after WWI but its ethical legacy continued in the CCF/NDP tradition

### 7.7 Temperance and Prohibition

- Alcohol was seen as a major social evil: poverty, domestic violence, lost wages
- **Women's Christian Temperance Union (WCTU)**: the leading temperance organization
- **Temperance vs. Prohibition**: temperance sought moderation; prohibition sought a legal ban
- The war provided the moral urgency to achieve prohibition (alcohol was wasteful, unpatriotic)
- **The Scott Act (1878)**: Canada Temperance Act; allowed local option votes on prohibition — an early example of direct democracy on moral questions
- Post-WWI: provincial governments replaced outright prohibition with **government control** of liquor sales — a more pragmatic, revenue-generating approach
- The prohibition experiment showed both the power of moral reform movements and the limits of legislation to change behaviour

### 7.8 Eugenics

- Eugenics: the belief that human populations could be "improved" through selective reproduction — sterilizing the "unfit" and encouraging reproduction among the "fit"
- Not fringe — it was mainstream science and policy in Canada in the 1920s–30s
- **Alberta's Sexual Sterilization Act (1928)**: authorized the sterilization of institutionalized people deemed "mentally defective" — thousands sterilized over several decades
- **BC also had sterilization legislation**
- Eugenics was deeply tied to racism, class bias, and ableism: the "unfit" were disproportionately poor, Indigenous, immigrant, or disabled
- **Leilani Muir** successfully sued the Alberta government in 1996 for her wrongful sterilization
- The eugenics movement shows how "progressive" reformers could hold deeply oppressive beliefs — it is essential to understanding the contradictions of early 20th-century Canadian reform

### 7.9 Reform Politics: Third Parties

- By the early 1920s, the old two-party (Liberal-Conservative) system was breaking down
- **Agrarian movements**: United Farmers of Ontario (UFO), United Farmers of Alberta (UFA) — protest movements against railway freight rates, grain elevator monopolies, and tariff policies that benefited central Canada
  - UFO won the Ontario election in 1919; UFA governed Alberta 1921–1935
- **Progressive Party (1921)**: Farmers ran federally; won 65 seats in 1921 — unprecedented for a third party
  - King managed to co-opt many Progressive MPs; the party largely dissolved back into the Liberals by the late 1920s
- **Co-operative Commonwealth Federation (CCF, 1932)**: merged agrarian, labour, and socialist streams; the **Regina Manifesto** called for the end of capitalism; key voice for social democracy
- **Social Credit (1935)**: Alberta; quirky monetary theories; anti-bank; deeply conservative in social matters despite "radical" economic theories
- **Bloc patterns**: parties representing regional or ideological interests became a permanent feature of Canadian politics

## UNIT 8: THE GREAT DEPRESSION

### 8.5 The Great Depression

*(See also 6.6 above — this section emphasizes economic and social dimensions)*

**Causes:**
- Over-speculation in stocks; unsustainable debt; weak banking regulation (US)
- Global credit contraction following the crash
- Canada's dependence on wheat exports made it especially vulnerable (wheat prices collapsed ~60%)
- High tariffs (including Smoot-Hawley in the US) strangled international trade

**The Scale of Suffering:**
- Unemployment: ~27% nationally; in some prairie towns, >50%
- Hundreds of thousands rode freight trains as hobos
- Drought + depression = human catastrophe on the prairies ("Dirty Thirties")
- Relief payments were minimal, humiliating, and administered by municipalities that were themselves bankrupt

**Political Responses:**
- **R.B. Bennett**: raised tariffs, tried relief camps, too little too late; his "New Deal" proposals (1935) were declared unconstitutional by the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council
- **King**: won 1935 on vague promises; established the **Royal Commission on Dominion-Provincial Relations (Rowell-Sirois Commission, 1937)**: recommended major federal-provincial restructuring — many recommendations implemented post-WWII
- **New social programs**: Unemployment Insurance finally established **1940** (a key recommendation accepted)

**Social and Cultural Impact:**
- The Depression shattered faith in unregulated capitalism
- Created a consensus across Canada that government had an obligation to provide social safety nets
- This consensus drove the post-WWII welfare state

> The Great Depression exposed the structural weaknesses of Canada’s export-dependent economy and constitutional division of powers, leading to widespread unemployment, regional hardship, and a lasting shift toward greater federal responsibility for social welfare.

## UNIT 9: POST-WAR CANADA, THE COLD WAR, AND THE TRUDEAU ERA

### 9.2 One Dominion (Post-War Canada)

- Canada emerged from WWII as a **middle power** — not a superpower, but significant economically, militarily, and diplomatically
- Population grew rapidly (baby boom + immigration)
- Economy boomed: pent-up consumer demand, US investment, resource exports
- Suburbanization accelerated; the car shaped urban form
- **Newfoundland joined Confederation 1949** (completing the country as it exists today)

### 9.3 The North: Economy and Territory

- The North (Yukon, Northwest Territories) was administered directly by the federal government — not a province
- Seen as a vast resource frontier: minerals, oil, gas, fur, fish
- The Cold War made the North strategically critical: it lay between the Soviet Union and the US
- **DEW Line (Distant Early Warning)**: radar stations across the Arctic (built 1954–57) — a US-funded project on Canadian soil; tensions over sovereignty
- Indigenous peoples of the North (Inuit, Dene, and others) were largely invisible in federal planning — their land was being used without consent
- Post-WWII resource extraction (nickel, uranium, oil) proceeded with little Indigenous input
- Northern sovereignty remains a live issue (especially as Arctic ice melts and Northwest Passage opens)

### 9.4 The Cold War

**Context:**
- Soviet-American rivalry (1945–1991) dominated international relations
- Nuclear weapons made the stakes existential
- Canada was caught between its southern neighbour (the US) and its loyalty to the Western alliance

**Igor Gouzenko (1945):**
- Soviet cipher clerk at the Soviet Embassy in Ottawa defected; revealed a Soviet spy network operating in Canada
- One of the opening shots of the Cold War
- Led to anti-communist anxiety in Canada; security screenings of civil servants

**NATO (1949):**
- Canada was a founding member of the North Atlantic Treaty Organization
- Lester Pearson (External Affairs minister) played a key role in its formation
- Canada hosted US forces; contributed troops to the Korean War (1950–53)

**NORAD (1957):**
- North American Aerospace Defense Command — joint Canada-US air defence
- Controversial: further entangled Canadian sovereignty with US military priorities

**Nuclear Weapons:**
- By the early 1960s, Canada agreed to accept American nuclear warheads on Canadian soil/delivery systems (Bomarc missiles, CF-101 aircraft)
- This became a huge political controversy under Diefenbaker (see 9.6)
- Anti-nuclear movement in Canada grew (Voice of Women, 1960)

### 9.5 Post-War Leadership and State-Making

**Social Programs Built Under King, St. Laurent, and Diefenbaker:**
- **Family Allowances ("baby bonus," 1945)**: universal monthly payments to mothers for each child — Canada's first universal social program
- **Unemployment Insurance (1940)**: contributed to by workers and employers
- **Old Age Security (1952)**: universal pension for Canadians 70+
- **Hospital Insurance (1957)**: Diefenbaker's government extended hospital coverage nationally — precursor to Medicare
- **Canada Pension Plan (1965)**: earnings-based supplement to OAS (under Pearson)
- **Medicare (1966–68)**: Universal health insurance — a central piece of the Canadian welfare state (see Pearson section)

**Louis St. Laurent (PM 1948–1957):**
- "Uncle Louis" — popular, grandfatherly, stable
- Oversaw economic boom, suburbanization, and foundation of the welfare state
- Cold War commitment: Korea, NATO
- Trans-Canada pipeline controversy (1956): pushed through pipeline legislation using closure → gave Diefenbaker an issue

### 9.6 Dief is the Chief

**John Diefenbaker (PM 1957–1963):**
- Prairie populist; champion of "the little guy"
- First significant Conservative win since 1930; won a massive majority in 1958
- **Canadian Bill of Rights (1960)**: statutory (not constitutional) protection of fundamental rights — a precursor to the 1982 Charter
- **Indigenous voting rights (1960)**: extended the federal vote to Status Indians — a significant, if long-delayed, step
- Cold War tensions: the **AVRO Arrow** cancellation (**1959**) — Canada's advanced jet interceptor program cancelled, 14,000 engineers laid off (many went to NASA); remains a sore point in Canadian history; represented the cost of Canada's dependence on US defence policy
- Nuclear weapons controversy: Diefenbaker had agreed to accept US nuclear warheads but then wavered; the US publicly contradicted him; his cabinet split; he lost a non-confidence vote and the 1963 election — Pearson promised to accept the warheads and won

### 9.7 The Pearson Interlude

**Lester B. Pearson (PM 1963–1968):**
- Nobel Peace Prize winner (1957) for creating the concept of UN peacekeeping (Suez Crisis, 1956)
- Led minority governments; worked with the NDP (under **Tommy Douglas**, "father of Medicare") to achieve major social programs

**Key Achievements:**
- **Medicare (Medical Care Act, 1966)**: Universal physician insurance implemented by 1968 — the cornerstone of Canadian social identity; built on Saskatchewan's provincial model (Tommy Douglas, 1962)
- **Canada Pension Plan (1965)**
- **New Canadian Flag (1965)**: The Maple Leaf flag replaced the old Red Ensign — deeply controversial among English Canadian Conservatives; a symbol of Canadian distinctiveness
- **Royal Commission on Bilingualism and Biculturalism (1963)**: Recognized that Canada's French-English divide required systematic attention; laid groundwork for the Official Languages Act (1969, under Trudeau)
- **Immigration Act reform (1967)**: Introduced the **points system** — replaced racial/ethnic preferences with skills, education, language criteria; opened immigration to non-European sources
- **Quiet Revolution response**: Pearson tried to accommodate Quebec nationalism; saw bilingualism as the key

### 9.8 Trudeau I

**Pierre Elliott Trudeau (PM 1968–1979, 1980–1984):**

**"Trudeaumania" (1968):**
- Rock star political celebrity; intellectual; unconventional; charismatic
- His vision: a Just Society; bilingualism; the Charter of Rights (eventually)

**Key Policies and Events:**
- **Official Languages Act (1969)**: Established French and English as official languages of the federal government; French Canadians could be served in French anywhere in Canada
- **Criminal Law Amendment Act (1969)**: Decriminalized homosexuality (between consenting adults in private) and liberalized abortion law — "The state has no place in the bedrooms of the nation"
- **War Measures Act and the October Crisis (1970)**: see 9.10 below
- **Foreign Investment Review Agency (FIRA, 1974)**: screened foreign (mainly US) investment
- **National Energy Program (NEP, 1980)**: Attempted to Canadianize the oil industry and redistribute oil revenues — hugely resented in Alberta as federal interference
- **Wage and Price Controls (1975)**: Anti-inflation measures — reversed his earlier opposition (he had called wage controls an "illusion" in the 1974 election, then imposed them)
- **Constitution Act, 1982**: Patriated the Constitution from Britain; included the **Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms** (universally applicable, override by notwithstanding clause in s.33); the amending formula; BUT Quebec never signed — a wound that would fester

### 9.9 Cold War Quebec

**The Quiet Revolution (1960s):**
- Under Quebec Liberal Premier **Jean Lesage**: "Maîtres chez nous" (Masters in our own house)
- **Nationalization of Hydro-Québec**: provinces took control of electrical companies → Hydro-Québec became a symbol of Quebec economic power
- Creation of a **Ministry of Education**: Church had controlled education; now the state took over — enormous shift
- Growth of the Quebec civil service; expansion of a French-speaking professional class
- Rapid secularization: church attendance plummeted; birth rate collapsed (ending the "revanche des berceaux" demographic strategy)

**French Canadian Nationalism:**
- The Quiet Revolution revealed how unequal French Canadians were in their own province (English controlled major corporations; French Canadians were systematically disadvantaged economically)
- **Three responses emerged:**
  1. **Federalism with bilingualism** (Trudeau's vision): French Canadians could be equal within Canada; no need for separation
  2. **Special status/distinct society for Quebec** (people like Lesage and later Bourassa): Canada must recognize Quebec as different within federation
  3. **Independence/sovereignty** (Lévesque and the Parti Québécois): Only a sovereign Quebec could fully protect and develop French Canadian culture and economy

**René Lévesque and the Parti Québécois:**
- PQ founded 1968; won Quebec provincial election **1976**
- Held a **referendum on sovereignty-association in 1980**: 60% voted NO
- Introduced **Bill 101 (Charter of the French Language, 1977)**: Made French the only official language in Quebec; restricted access to English schools; required French on all commercial signage — fundamentally reshaped Quebec's linguistic landscape

### 9.10 The October Crisis

**Background:**
- **Front de Libération du Québec (FLQ)**: Marxist separatist terrorist organization formed in 1963
- Carried out over 200 bombings, robberies, and violent acts through the 1960s
- Their ultimate goal: Quebec independence through armed revolution

**October 1970:**
- **October 5**: FLQ kidnapped **James Cross**, British trade commissioner in Montreal
- **October 10**: FLQ kidnapped **Pierre Laporte**, Quebec's Minister of Labour (and Immigration)
- Kidnappers demanded the release of 23 FLQ "political prisoners," a ransom, and broadcast of their manifesto (the CBC aired the manifesto)
- Quebec Premier **Robert Bourassa** and Montreal Mayor **Jean Drapeau** formally requested federal intervention

**Trudeau's Response:**
- **October 16, 1970**: Trudeau invoked the **War Measures Act** — the first peacetime use in Canadian history
- Suspended civil liberties; RCMP and police arrested ~450 people (most without charge; most released quickly)
- **Trudeau's famous "just watch me"** response to a journalist asking how far he would go
- **October 17**: Pierre Laporte's body found in the trunk of a car — he had been murdered by the FLQ
- James Cross was released in December 1970 in exchange for safe passage of his kidnappers to Cuba
- FLQ cells rounded up; the organization effectively destroyed

**Controversy:**
- Was the War Measures Act necessary? The FLQ had no capacity for insurrection
- Civil liberties groups, the NDP (under David Lewis, and earlier Tommy Douglas called it "a sledgehammer to crack a peanut"), and many Quebecers were horrified by the suspension of habeas corpus
- The majority of Canadians supported Trudeau's actions — polls showed ~87% approval
- The October Crisis discredited separatist violence; it helped marginalize the radical fringe but may have also hardened resolve for constitutional solutions to Quebec's grievances
- **Legacy**: the Emergency Powers Act (1988) replaced the War Measures Act with more limited peacetime emergency powers

## ESSAY PREPARATION: KEY THEMES AND ARGUMENTS

### Theme 1: French-English Relations
A consistent thread from Confederation to the October Crisis. Key turning points: 1885 (Riel), Boer War, WWI conscription, WWII conscription, Quiet Revolution, October Crisis. The tension between two "founding peoples" visions and Trudeau's individual rights/bilingualism alternative.

### Theme 2: Indigenous Peoples and the Canadian State
Dispossession through treaties (not fairly implemented), the Indian Act, residential schools, the pass system, 1885, military service without equal benefits, sterilization (eugenics), voting rights only in 1960. Canada built its prosperity on Indigenous lands and systematically destroyed Indigenous cultures and societies.

### Theme 3: The Expanding Role of the State
From the minimalist colonial state to the National Policy, the War Measures Act in WWI, Bennett's New Deal attempt, wartime mobilization in WWII, and then the post-war welfare state (baby bonus, OAS, UI, Medicare, CPP). Canada moved from a liberal laissez-faire model to a social democratic mixed economy over roughly 1867–1968.

### Theme 4: Canada's Search for Autonomy
Moved from British colony to independent state. Milestones: Boer War compromise, separate signature on Versailles (1919), Statute of Westminster (1931), separate declaration of war (1939), Ogdensburg (pivot to US), Suez Crisis (Pearson's peacekeeping), Constitution Act 1982 (patriation). Independence from Britain led to new dependence on the US.

### Theme 5: Immigration, Race, and Nation-Building
The National Policy's immigration program was never race-neutral. Chinese head taxes, Asian exclusion, anti-Black discrimination, Japanese Canadian internment, Jewish refugee refusal, residential schools — all show how "Canadian" was defined in racial terms. The 1967 points system represents a significant policy shift toward formal race-neutrality.

### Theme 6: Women's Roles and Rights
Moved from exclusion from public life to full (formal) equality. Key moments: WCTU and temperance, WWI industrial work, 1918 federal vote, Persons Case (1929), WWII military service, post-war "back to the home" pressure, finally Medicare and women's liberation. Formal rights often exceeded lived reality.

### Theme 7: Regional Discontent and Third Parties
The National Policy benefited central Canada; the West and Maritimes felt exploited. This drove third-party politics: Progressives, CCF, Social Credit, Union Nationale. Canada's multi-party system reflects genuine regional and ideological diversity that the two old parties could not contain.
